Step 1

Tackle credit card debt first.

There is no point earning 7–10% per year in the market if you are paying 18–29% on a credit card balance. The math says: get the debt under control before you start investing.

🏦

1. Consolidate what you can

Move high-interest balances to your lowest-rate option first. A line of credit, balance-transfer card, or consolidation loan can reduce the interest bleeding while you pay things down. Less interest means more of your payment actually reduces what you owe.

🎯

2. Clear the easiest one first

Pay off your smallest debt as fast as possible. This is not about optimizing interest — it is about building momentum. Crossing one debt off the list gives you the confidence to keep going.

📉

3. Then attack by interest rate

Once you have momentum, order your remaining debts from highest to lowest interest rate. Throw every extra dollar at the highest-rate debt while making minimum payments on the rest. When the highest-rate debt dies, roll that payment into the next one.

While you are paying down debt, keep your credit cards available as a temporary safety net for true emergencies. Once the high-interest debt is gone, the next step is building a real cash buffer so you never need to fall back on credit.

Footnotes

The hybrid approach — consolidate, then smallest-first for momentum, then highest-interest — balances psychological progress with mathematical efficiency. This is educational content, not personal debt advice. Compare consolidation options, interest rates, and terms before acting.

Step 2

Build a rainy day fund — after the debt is gone.

Once high-interest debt is cleared, the next priority is cash you can reach without selling investments or running up a credit card balance.

🛟

Start with one month of expenses

Aim to keep roughly one month of essential spending in a high-interest savings account or cash ETF. This covers the surprise car repair, dental bill, or short job gap without forcing you to touch your investments.

📈

Build toward 3–6 months

Over time, grow the buffer to 3–6 months of expenses. The right number depends on your job stability, family situation, and comfort level. A larger buffer means more room to ride out market downturns without selling.

💳

Until the debt is gone, use credit as backup

While you are still clearing high-interest debt, it can make sense to keep a credit card available for genuine emergencies — because every dollar you save in cash instead of paying down 20%+ debt is costing you. Once the debt is cleared, build the real cash buffer so you never need to fall back on credit again.

Footnotes

The conventional guidance (Dave Ramsey, etc.) suggests a small starter emergency fund of $1,000–$2,000 before attacking debt, then a full 3–6 month fund after. The key principle: do not invest money you might need for short-term emergencies. A cash buffer protects your long-term investments from forced selling during downturns.

Step 3

Set a financial target — then work backwards.

Knowing what you are investing for changes everything: which account to use, how much to contribute, and how long the money needs to grow.

When to withdraw

Sell because the money has a job, not because the market is loud.

Selling can make sense when the money is needed for a planned goal, an emergency, rebalancing, or reducing risk before a known expense.

What usually hurts long-term investors is panic selling: selling only because the market dropped and the chart feels scary. Review your goal, timeline, and investment mix before reacting.

For Canadians: TFSA withdrawals restore your contribution room in the next calendar year, so money you take out for a real goal can be replaced later. That flexibility can make a TFSA useful for long-term investing when it fits your situation.

🏠

A real goal

Home down payment, education, a planned purchase, or another clear use.

🆘

During emergencies

Unexpected urgent expenses can happen. Ideally, use cash savings first.

🚫

Not market reaction

A market drop alone is not a plan. Review your goal, timeline, and investment mix before reacting.

Footnotes

Selling can be appropriate when it matches a goal, rebalancing plan, or changing time horizon. The behaviour to avoid is panic selling only because the market fell.

Income target

How much invested could support the income you want?

The 4% rule is a simple retirement-planning shortcut: multiply the annual portfolio income you want by 25. That gives you an investment target — the portfolio size you'd need to draw that income at a 4% withdrawal rate.

Use this as a conservative scale estimate, then compare it with your current balance, monthly investing habit, and return assumption.

Footnotes

The 4% rule is a simplified retirement-planning heuristic based on a fixed withdrawal rate. Taxes, fees, inflation, account rules, and personal circumstances can change outcomes. This is educational content, not personal financial advice.

Step 4

Create the right account for your goal.

Not all accounts are the same. TFSA, FHSA, RRSP, RESP, and taxable accounts each serve different purposes — and the wrong choice can cost you in taxes, penalties, or lost flexibility.

For Canadian investors

Why many Canadians start with a TFSA

For many investors, maxing out a TFSA first makes sense — not just for the tax savings, but for the flexibility. Unlike an FHSA or RRSP, TFSA withdrawals have no penalties regardless of what you use the money for.

An FHSA gives you a tax deduction and tax-free withdrawals for a first home — but if life changes and you need the money for something else, you may face tax consequences. An RRSP also gives a deduction, but withdrawals are taxed as income and can trigger withholding tax.

The question to ask: is locking the money into a specific-purpose account worth the tax benefit, given that life can change? For many people, the TFSA's flexibility makes it the smarter first account.

TFSA contribution room is personal. Withdrawals generally restore room in the next calendar year. Confirm your official room with CRA My Account.

Moderate leverage

1.25× ETFs: leveraged growth without margin debt

A 1.25× leveraged ETF aims to deliver 125% of the daily return of its underlying index — so if the index rises 1%, the ETF targets roughly 1.25%. This is a modest boost compared to 2× or 3× leveraged products, and crucially, it does not involve borrowing money or putting up collateral.

📊

1.25× leveraged ETF

Uses derivatives inside the fund to target 1.25× daily index returns. You buy shares like any other ETF. No margin account, no collateral, no risk of a margin call. Your maximum loss is the amount you invested in that ETF.

✅

No margin account needed

With a 1.25× ETF, you do not open a margin account, do not borrow money from your broker, do not post collateral, and never face a margin call. You simply buy shares of the ETF the same way you would buy any other holding — no debt, no forced selling, and your loss is limited to what you own in that specific ETF.

🧠

What to know before investing

Leveraged ETFs amplify both gains and losses. A 1.25× ETF will fall roughly 1.25× as much on down days. Over long periods, daily reset effects (volatility decay) can cause returns to drift from a simple 1.25× multiple. These are tools for investors who understand the mechanics and can tolerate the extra volatility.

Source: Global X Enhanced Growth ETFs ↗. All leveraged ETFs involve risk. Review the prospectus, MER, and daily reset mechanics before investing.

Footnotes

Leveraged ETFs are not suitable for all investors. Returns can deviate significantly from the stated multiple over periods longer than one day due to compounding and volatility. These are examples for research, not recommendations. Compare fees, holdings, risk rating, and whether the product fits your time horizon and risk tolerance.

Timing risk

Trying to time the noise is harder than it looks.

The problem is not intelligence. The problem is emotion, uncertainty, and consistency. DCA removes the pressure of deciding whether today is the perfect day to invest.

For people investing from paycheques, DCA is a natural fit because the money arrives over time. If you receive a large lump sum, you can invest it all at once or phase it in based on your comfort level — but either way, the key habit is to keep investing from income afterward.

Sources: Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, "Do Individual Day Traders Make Money?"; RBC GAM, "Benefits of investing regularly and dollar cost averaging"

FAQ

Common dollar-cost averaging questions

What is dollar-cost averaging?

Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule. When prices are lower, the same amount buys more units. When prices are higher, it buys fewer units.

Should I keep DCA investing when the market is down?

If your time horizon, emergency fund, debt situation, and risk tolerance still support long-term investing, a fixed DCA schedule can help you avoid emotional timing decisions. If your personal situation has changed, review the plan before contributing more.

Does DCA mean buying the dip?

Not exactly. DCA means investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule. Lower prices naturally buy more units, but the point is not to guess the bottom. The point is to keep the habit steady.

Is daily DCA better during volatile markets?

Daily DCA spreads contributions across the most market days, but weekly, biweekly, payday-based, and monthly investing can also work. The key is to keep investing at a rate you can automate and sustain.

What if I feel nervous investing during a crash?

Nervousness is a signal to review your plan, not automatically abandon it. Check your cash buffer, debt, time horizon, contribution amount, and investment mix. If the plan still fits, the schedule can keep going.

Can I dollar-cost average in a TFSA?

Yes. Many Canadians use a TFSA for recurring ETF investments when they have contribution room. Confirm your official TFSA room and account fit before contributing.

What ETFs do Canadians use for DCA?

Many Canadians research broad-market or all-in-one ETFs for long-term DCA. Examples include Canadian-listed ETFs that cover U.S., Canadian, or global markets. Compare fees, holdings, risk rating, and account fit before choosing.

What is the Rule of 72?

The Rule of 72 estimates how long money takes to double: 72 divided by the annual return equals the approximate number of years.

Is DCA better than lump sum investing?

If you already have a large lump sum, investing sooner has often performed better historically because markets tend to rise over time. But DCA may be easier emotionally and can help investors avoid sitting in cash out of fear.

What if I suddenly have a large lump sum?

You can invest it all at once or use a temporary fixed DCA schedule to phase it in. The important part is that the lump sum does not replace the ongoing habit: keep investing from income afterward if your budget allows.

What return should I assume in the calculator?

Use conservative assumptions and test multiple scenarios. A higher assumption makes the output look better, but it does not make the result more likely.

Are ETF tickers on this site recommendations?

No. ETF tickers are examples for research. The right choice depends on your account, time horizon, fees, tax situation, currency exposure, and comfort with market swings.

Do foreign withholding taxes apply inside a TFSA?

Canadian TFSA growth and withdrawals are generally tax-free for Canadian tax purposes, but foreign withholding tax can still apply to some foreign dividends, especially U.S. dividends.

What is the 4% rule?

The 4% rule is a simplified retirement-planning shortcut that estimates a portfolio target by dividing desired annual income by 4%. It is a planning rule of thumb, not a guarantee.
